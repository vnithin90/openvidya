Projectile motion answers the question *where is the particle at time t*. This
concept answers a different kind of question entirely: *what would happen to a
charge placed here?* — asked simultaneously at every point in space, whether or
not anything is there to experience it.

That difference is not a detail. There is no initial condition here, no time
evolution, nothing to integrate forward. The model is a rule, and the object it
produces is a field.

## Superposition is the whole trick

Each charge produces its own field, and where several charges are present the
fields simply add — as vectors, point by point.

$$
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_i \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\, \frac{q_i\,(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i)}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i|^3}
$$

Choose **like-pair** and look at the midpoint. Two equal charges push in exactly
opposite directions there, so the field is precisely zero — a charge released at
that point would feel nothing at all. Move one charge and the null point moves
with it.

## Reading distance from the arrows

Choose **dipole** and move the probe outward. Far from a single isolated charge
the field falls as $1/r^2$. Far from a dipole it falls as $1/r^3$ — faster,
because the two opposite charges increasingly cancel as you retreat and only
their separation still registers.

That difference in exponent is measurable, and it is one of the checks in the
test suite: the far-field slope on a log–log plot must come out at $-2$ for a
net-charged configuration and $-3$ for a neutral one.

## What the picture is not telling you

Field lines are seductive and it is worth naming what they are not.

They are **not trajectories**. A charge released into this field does not travel
along a field line, any more than a projectile travels along the direction of
gravity. The line gives the direction of the *force*, and force sets
acceleration, not velocity. A charge released at rest starts off along the line
and then, in general, curves away from it.

The **arrow length is a choice**, not a measurement. In this visualization every
arrow is drawn the same length and coloured by magnitude, because $|\mathbf{E}|$
grows without bound near a charge — drawn to scale, a single arrow near a charge
would be kilometres long and everything else would be invisible.

And this is a **flat slice** through a three-dimensional field. The apparent
density of lines in the plane is not a reliable guide to magnitude in 3D.

## How we know the code is right

The implementation adds up Coulomb terms. The tests never do. Instead they check
things that follow from the model without recomputing it: the flux of
$\mathbf{E}$ through a closed surface,

$$
\oint \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

evaluated as a numerical surface integral; the vanishing of $\oint\mathbf{E}
\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ around a closed loop; and the relation $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla
V$ with the potential summed separately and differentiated numerically.

If the exponent in Coulomb's law were 2 instead of 3 in the denominator, or the
constant were wrong, or a sign were flipped, the flux integral would not land on
$Q/\varepsilon_0$. That is what makes it a test rather than a restatement.
