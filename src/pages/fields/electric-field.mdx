Projectile motion answers the question *where is the particle at time t*. This
concept answers a different kind of question entirely: *what would happen to a
charge placed here?* — asked at every point in space at once. It is asked whether or not anything is there to feel it.

That difference is not a detail. There is no starting condition here. Nothing changes with time. There is nothing to step forward. The model is a rule, and the object it
produces is a field.

## Superposition is the whole trick

Each charge produces its own field. Where several charges are present, the fields simply add. They add as vectors, point by point.

$$
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_i \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\, \frac{q_i\,(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i)}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i|^3}
$$

Choose **like-pair** and look at the midpoint. Two equal charges push in exactly opposite directions there. So the field is precisely zero. A charge released at that point would feel nothing at all. Move one charge and the null point moves
with it.

## Reading distance from the arrows

Choose **dipole** and move the probe outward. Far from a single isolated charge
the field falls as $1/r^2$. Far from a dipole it falls as $1/r^3$, which is faster. The two opposite charges cancel more and more as you retreat. Only their separation still registers.

That difference in exponent is measurable. It is one of the checks in the test suite. On a log–log plot the far-field slope must come out at $-2$ for a net-charged configuration, and $-3$ for a neutral one.

## What the picture is not telling you

Field lines are seductive and it is worth naming what they are not.

They are **not trajectories**. A charge released into this field does not travel along
a field line. A thrown ball does not travel straight down either, and gravity points
straight down. The line gives the direction of the *force*. Force sets acceleration,
not velocity. A charge released at rest starts off along the line. After that it
usually curves away from it.

The **arrow length is a choice**, not a measurement. In this visualization every arrow is drawn the same length and coloured by magnitude. The reason is that $|\mathbf{E}|$ grows without bound near a charge. Drawn to scale, one arrow near a charge would be kilometres long, and everything else would be invisible.

And this is a **flat slice** through a three-dimensional field. The apparent
density of lines in the plane is not a reliable guide to magnitude in 3D.

## How we know the code is right

The implementation adds up Coulomb terms. The tests never do. Instead they check
things that follow from the model without recomputing it: the flux of
$\mathbf{E}$ through a closed surface,

$$
\oint \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

evaluated as a numerical surface integral. Second, that $\oint\mathbf{E} \cdot
d\mathbf{l}$ vanishes around a closed loop. Third, the relation $\mathbf{E} =
-\nabla V$, with the potential summed separately and differentiated numerically.

Suppose the exponent in Coulomb's law were 2 instead of 3 in the denominator. Or the constant were wrong. Or a sign were flipped. Then the flux integral would not land on $Q/\varepsilon_0$. That is what makes it a test rather than a restatement.
