Throw something. Once it has left your hand, only one force acts on it, and that
force points straight down. Nothing pushes it forwards. Yet it keeps moving
forwards. That is the whole of projectile motion. It is worth sitting with for a moment. Most people's intuition says otherwise.

## The two motions are independent

Watch the green arrow while the animation runs. It never changes. Gravity acts downwards, so it changes only the downward component of velocity. It has no horizontal component, and therefore no horizontal effect.

$$
\frac{d v_x}{dt} = 0, \qquad \frac{d v_y}{dt} = -g
$$

So the sideways motion is steady. The up-and-down motion speeds up at a fixed rate. The two run side by side and never affect each other. Everything
else follows from this.

Set the launch angle to 90° and the range collapses to zero. All the velocity went into the vertical component. Nothing is left to carry the particle forward. Set it to 0° and the range collapses again, for the opposite reason. There is no upward part, so the ball never leaves the ground.

## Where the maximum lies

Somewhere between those two failures is a best angle. Tick **compare angles**
and look at the pairs: 15° and 75° reach the same distance. So do 30° and 60°.

$$
R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}
$$

Since $\sin 2\theta = \sin(180° - 2\theta)$, the range at $\theta$ equals the
range at $90° - \theta$. The two are complementary, and the maximum sits exactly
where they meet, at 45°.

This symmetry is a property of the *model*, not of the world. Throw a real
javelin at 45° and you will not get the maximum distance, because a real javelin
experiences drag and lift. The symmetry is the first thing air resistance
destroys.

## Try changing gravity

Use the Moon and Jupiter buttons. The trajectory keeps its shape. It is always a parabola. But it stretches and shrinks. Range and flight time both scale as
$1/g$; maximum height does too.

Notice what does not change. The sideways speed is still constant. Going up still takes exactly as long as coming down. Those are consequences of
the structure of the equations, not of the value of $g$.

## What would break this

The model assumes no air resistance. That assumption is doing far more work than
it appears to. Drag is not a small correction for most real projectiles. For a tennis ball or a badminton shuttle it dominates. The trajectory becomes visibly asymmetric, and the best launch angle drops well below 45°. The parabola is a
good description of a shot put and a poor description of a ping-pong ball.

This is not a flaw to apologise for. It is what a physical model *is*: a
deliberately constrained representation, useful exactly as far as its
assumptions hold, and no further. The panel below lists those limits. It also shows the evidence that the code obeys the law it claims to.
