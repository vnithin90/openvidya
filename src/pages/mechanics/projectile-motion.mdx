Throw something. Once it has left your hand, only one force acts on it, and that
force points straight down. Nothing pushes it forwards. Yet it keeps moving
forwards. That is the whole of projectile motion, and it is worth sitting with
for a moment, because it contradicts what most people's intuition suggests.

## The two motions are independent

Watch the green arrow while the animation runs. It never changes. Gravity acts
downwards, so it changes only the downward component of velocity — it has no
horizontal component and therefore no horizontal effect.

$$
\frac{d v_x}{dt} = 0, \qquad \frac{d v_y}{dt} = -g
$$

So the horizontal motion is uniform, and the vertical motion is uniformly
accelerated, and the two proceed side by side without interacting. Everything
else follows from this.

Set the launch angle to 90° and the range collapses to zero — all the velocity
went into the vertical component and there is nothing left to carry the particle
forward. Set it to 0° and the range collapses again for the opposite reason: no
vertical component, so the particle never leaves the ground.

## Where the maximum lies

Somewhere between those two failures is a best angle. Tick **compare angles**
and look at the pairs: 15° and 75° reach the same distance. So do 30° and 60°.

$$
R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}
$$

Since $\sin 2\theta = \sin(180° - 2\theta)$, the range at $\theta$ equals the
range at $90° - \theta$. The two are complementary, and the maximum sits exactly
where they meet, at 45°.

This symmetry is a property of the *model*, not of the world. Throw a real
javelin at 45° and you will not get the maximum distance, because a real javelin
experiences drag and lift. The symmetry is the first thing air resistance
destroys.

## Try changing gravity

Use the Moon and Jupiter buttons. The trajectory keeps its shape — it is always
a parabola — but it stretches and shrinks. Range and flight time both scale as
$1/g$; maximum height does too.

Notice what does *not* change: the horizontal velocity is still constant, and
the ascent still takes exactly as long as the descent. Those are consequences of
the structure of the equations, not of the value of $g$.

## What would break this

The model assumes no air resistance. That assumption is doing far more work than
it appears to. Drag is not a small correction for most real projectiles — for a
tennis ball or a badminton shuttle it dominates, the trajectory becomes visibly
asymmetric, and the optimum launch angle drops well below 45°. The parabola is a
good description of a shot put and a poor description of a ping-pong ball.

This is not a flaw to apologise for. It is what a physical model *is*: a
deliberately constrained representation, useful exactly as far as its
assumptions hold, and no further. The panel below states those constraints
explicitly, together with the evidence that the code obeys the law it claims to.
